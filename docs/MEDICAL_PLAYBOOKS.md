# Medical playbooks: emergencies for two elderly parents in India

Written for a lay family. Medically accurate to current guidelines as of September 2026, but **not a substitute for the parents' own doctor**. Items marked [REGION-DEPENDENT] or [UNCERTAIN] must be checked locally. The short, on-screen version of this lives in `hub/hub/playbooks.yaml` and is shown to every responder on the alert page. Review both with the parents' physician and edit for their actual medicines.

Context assumed: mother is diabetic, father has high blood pressure, both 60+, secluded house, ambulance 30-60 minutes away.

## Rules that apply to almost every emergency

- **Call for help first (112 or 108), then act.** Give the address, landmarks, and the patient's condition (Part 3).
- **Note the time symptoms started.** Hospital treatment for stroke and heart attack is time-limited, and "last seen well" is the first thing doctors ask.
- **Never give food, drink, or tablets to someone who is drowsy, confused, or unconscious.** They can choke.
- **Oxygen is not a general remedy.** It helps only when the pulse oximeter shows low SpO2 (Part 2).

## Part 1: the most common life-threatening emergencies

### 1. Heart attack (myocardial infarction)
- **Signs:** Pressure, tightness or heaviness in the centre of the chest lasting more than a few minutes; pain spreading to arm, jaw, neck or back; sweating, nausea, breathlessness, light-headedness. In elderly and diabetic people the classic chest pain is often absent: sudden unexplained breathlessness, extreme weakness, "gas-like" upper-abdominal discomfort, or collapse may be the only clue.
- **First 10 minutes:** Call 112/108. Sit the person down, semi-reclining, keep them still and calm. If not allergic to aspirin, no recent bleeding, and able to swallow, give one non-coated aspirin ~300 mg (in India commonly sold as 75/150 mg tablets: give two 150 mg or four 75 mg) **chewed**, not swallowed whole ([2025 ACC/AHA ACS guideline: 162-325 mg loading dose](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2024.11.009)). If they have their own prescribed nitroglycerin (sorbitrate/Isordil sublingual), help them take it ([AHA/Red Cross First Aid 2024](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001281)). If they become unresponsive and stop breathing normally, start CPR (Part 5).
- **Do NOT:** Let them walk to the vehicle; give nitroglycerin if they have taken sildenafil/tadalafil in the last 24-48 h or if they look faint/grey (low BP); give oxygen "just in case" if SpO2 is 90-92% or higher.
- **Golden window:** For STEMI, artery opened by angioplasty within 90 minutes of hospital arrival, or clot-busting drug within 30 minutes if no cath lab. Benefit falls sharply beyond 12 hours ([Indian door-to-balloon audit](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41998490/); [STEMI care in India](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4382554/)). [REGION-DEPENDENT] Find out now which hospital within 60-90 minutes has a 24x7 cath lab, and which nearer hospital can give thrombolysis; [STEMI India](https://www.stemiindia.com/) lists programme hospitals in several states.
- **Home oxygen?** Only if SpO2 < 90%. Trials (AVOID, DETO2X-AMI) show no benefit and possible harm from routine oxygen in heart attack ([JACC Advances review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9555075/)).

### 2. Stroke
- **Signs, BE FAST:** **B**alance suddenly lost; **E**yes, sudden loss of vision; **F**ace drooping on one side (ask them to smile); **A**rm weakness (one arm drifts down when both raised); **S**peech slurred or strange; **T**ime, call immediately and note the time ([American Stroke Association](https://www.stroke.org/en/help-and-support/resource-library/be-fast-materials/be-fast-infographic)). Also: sudden worst-ever headache, sudden confusion.
- **First 10 minutes:** Call 112/108 and say the word "stroke". Lay them down with head and shoulders slightly raised; if drowsy or vomiting, on their side. Note "last seen normal" time. Loosen clothing. Bring the medication list.
- **Do NOT:** Give food, water, aspirin or BP tablets (some strokes are bleeds, where aspirin worsens things and only a CT scan can tell); wait to "see if it passes". Even symptoms that resolve (TIA) need same-day assessment.
- **Golden window:** IV thrombolysis within 4.5 hours of onset; mechanical clot retrieval up to 6-24 h in selected cases. In India only ~20% of patients reach hospital within 4.5 h and ~11% of eligible patients get thrombolysis; rural centres far less ([ICMR data via The Week](https://www.theweek.in/news/health/2026/04/27/indias-growing-stroke-crisis-why-ncd-control-must-go-beyond-metro-centres.html); [Ludhiana rural study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33951647/)). [REGION-DEPENDENT] Identify the nearest hospital with 24x7 CT scan and a doctor who thrombolyses. This may not be the nearest hospital.
- **Home oxygen?** Not unless SpO2 < 94%.

### 3. Hypoglycaemia (low sugar): the most likely emergency for the mother
- **Signs:** Sweating, shakiness, hunger, palpitations, then confusion, odd behaviour, slurred speech, drowsiness, seizures, unconsciousness. Elderly people and those on beta-blockers (often prescribed for BP) may skip the warning signs and go straight to confusion. Glucometer < 70 mg/dL confirms; < 54 mg/dL is serious.
- **First 10 minutes (conscious, can swallow):** "Rule of 15": 15 g fast sugar (3-4 glucose tablets, 3 teaspoons sugar or glucose powder in water, half a glass of fruit juice or regular cold drink), recheck in 15 min, repeat if still < 70, then a snack ([ADA Standards of Care 2026](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S132/163927/6-Glycemic-Goals-Hypoglycemia-and-Hyperglycemic)).
- **Unconscious or unable to swallow safely:** Call 112/108, recovery position, glucagon injection if available (Part 6). Do NOT put sugar, honey or liquid in the mouth ([ADA glucagon guidance](https://diabetes.org/living-with-diabetes/hypoglycemia-low-blood-glucose/how-use-glucagon)). [UNCERTAIN] Glucose gel rubbed inside the cheek absorbs poorly and risks choking; last resort only if help is far away and no glucagon.
- **Watch out:** Hypoglycaemia from sulfonylureas (glimepiride, gliclazide, glibenclamide, very common in India) can recur for 24 hours or more; hospital observation is usually needed even if she wakes up.
- **Oxygen:** Not indicated.

### 4. Hyperglycaemia / DKA / HHS
- **Signs:** For a type 2 diabetic in her 60s the dangerous version is Hyperosmolar Hyperglycaemic State (HHS): builds over days, sugar often > 600 mg/dL (meter may read "HI"), extreme thirst, lots of urine, dry mouth, weakness, leg cramps, then confusion, drowsiness, seizures. Usually triggered by infection, missed medicines, or a heat wave ([Johns Hopkins](https://hopkinsdiabetesinfo.org/diabetic-emergencies-hyperosmolar-hyperglycemic-state-hhs/)). DKA (deep sighing breathing, fruity breath, vomiting, abdominal pain) is less common in type 2 but possible, especially on SGLT2 drugs (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin), even with near-normal sugar.
- **First 10 minutes:** Any sugar > 300 mg/dL with vomiting, drowsiness, confusion, or inability to keep fluids down = hospital now. Sips of plain water if fully alert. Take the glucometer and medicine list.
- **Do NOT:** Give extra insulin without a doctor's instruction; give sugary drinks; delay because "she seems just tired". Confusion in a diabetic elder is an emergency until proved otherwise.
- **Oxygen:** No.

### 5. Hypertensive crisis
- **Signs:** BP >= 180/120 mmHg. It is an **emergency** only with symptoms of organ damage: chest pain, breathlessness, severe headache, blurred vision, confusion, weakness/numbness, slurred speech, seizure. Without symptoms it is "urgency" ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/24470-hypertensive-crisis); [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/high-blood-pressure/expert-answers/hypertensive-crisis/faq-20058491)).
- **First 10 minutes:** If >= 180/120 **with any symptom**: call 112/108. If **no symptoms**: sit quietly 5 minutes, recheck with correct cuff position; if still >= 180/120, phone the doctor the same day and confirm the usual dose was taken.
- **Do NOT:** Give extra or double doses of BP tablets, or crush nifedipine under the tongue. Rapid drops in BP can cause stroke or heart attack ([Merck Manual](https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/cardiovascular-disorders/hypertension/hypertensive-emergencies)). Do not stop BP tablets abruptly either.
- **Oxygen:** Only if breathless with low SpO2.

### 6. Falls and hip fracture
- **Signs of hip fracture:** Unable to stand or bear weight after a fall, pain in hip/groin, injured leg looks shorter or turned outward ([AAOS](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/diseases--conditions/hip-fractures/)). Check for head injury (especially on blood thinners/aspirin/clopidogrel), confusion, vomiting.
- **First 10 minutes:** Do not rush to lift them. Severe pain, deformity, or cannot move a leg: **leave them where they are**, support the leg with cushions in the position found, keep them warm, call an ambulance. If uninjured, help them up slowly: side, hands and knees, chair ([NHS Falls](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/falls/)).
- **Do NOT:** Drag them, "test" the leg, or leave them on the floor for hours. A "long lie" over 1-2 hours causes dehydration, pressure sores, hypothermia, muscle breakdown and kidney damage ([Physiopedia](https://www.physio-pedia.com/Long_Lie)). This is the single strongest argument for a wearable SOS/fall-alert device and a daily check-in call.
- **Golden window:** Surgery within 24-48 hours. Any fall with head strike on blood thinners needs a CT scan.

### 7. Breathing difficulty (COPD flare, pneumonia, heart failure)
- **Signs:** Breathless at rest or unable to speak full sentences, breathing rate > 30/min, lips/fingertips blue or grey, SpO2 <= 90% (or a fall of 3-4 points from usual), new confusion, fever with cough, frothy pink sputum, swollen legs. Elderly pneumonia often presents as confusion or falls without fever.
- **First 10 minutes:** Sit upright, leaning slightly forward; loosen clothing; prescribed inhaler (salbutamol) with spacer; call 112/108 if any red flag. Start oxygen per Part 2.
- **Do NOT:** Lay them flat; give sedatives or codeine cough syrups; turn oxygen to high flow in a known COPD patient.
- **Oxygen:** Yes. This is the one category where home oxygen genuinely buys time, with targets (Part 2).

### 8. Choking
- **Signs:** Clutching throat, unable to speak/cough/breathe, going blue. Elderly people with dentures, dry mouth, or after stroke are at high risk.
- **First 10 minutes:** If they can cough, encourage coughing. If not: 5 hard back blows between the shoulder blades, then 5 abdominal thrusts, alternating until the object comes out or they collapse. If they become unresponsive, lower to the floor, call 112, start CPR ([Red Cross](https://www.redcross.org/take-a-class/resources/learn-first-aid/adult-child-choking)). Use chest thrusts if you cannot get your arms around them.
- **Do NOT:** Blind finger sweeps; give water; slap the back of someone still coughing effectively.

### 9. Cardiac arrest
- **Signs:** Collapses, unresponsive, not breathing or only gasping/snoring irregularly. Agonal breathing is present in 40-60% of arrests and is often mistaken for "still breathing" ([AHA 2025 Adult BLS](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001369)).
- **First 10 minutes:** Shout for help, call 112/108 on speaker, start hands-only CPR immediately (Part 5), use an AED if one exists. Survival drops roughly 7-10% per minute without CPR.
- **Do NOT:** Check for a pulse (lay people get it wrong), splash water, wait for the ambulance before starting.

### 10. Sepsis
- **Signs in elders:** New confusion or unusual sleepiness, very fast breathing, fast heartbeat, fever **or** abnormally low temperature, cold/mottled/pale skin, very little urine, shivering, plus any infection source (urine, chest, diabetic foot ulcer, skin) ([Sepsis Alliance](https://www.sepsis.org/sepsisand/aging/)). Diabetics are at much higher risk.
- **First 10 minutes:** Hospital, not the local clinic. Mortality rises ~8% for every hour antibiotics are delayed.
- **Do NOT:** Start leftover antibiotics at home and wait; dismiss confusion as "age".

### 11. Dehydration and heat stroke
- **Signs:** Heat exhaustion: heavy sweating, weakness, cramps, nausea, dizziness. Heat stroke: body temperature >= 40 C, hot skin (dry or sweaty), confusion, staggering, seizures, unconsciousness. Peaks April-June; diuretics and SGLT2 diabetes drugs raise risk ([CDC](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/138337/cdc_138337_DS1.pdf)).
- **First 10 minutes:** Shade/cooler/AC, remove excess clothing, cool aggressively (wet the skin, fan, ice packs to neck, armpits, groin); if fully alert, sips of ORS/water. Confused or unconscious: call 112/108, cool while waiting, nothing by mouth.
- **Do NOT:** Give paracetamol for heat stroke; give fluids to a confused person; leave them alone.

### 12. Fainting (syncope)
- **First 10 minutes:** Lay flat, raise legs, loosen clothing; check breathing. Most recover within a minute.
- **Red flags, call ambulance:** Not awake within 1-2 minutes, fainted with chest pain or palpitations, during exertion or while lying down, jerking movements, head injury, or any faint in someone over 65 with known heart disease. Same-day medical review even after full recovery.

## Part 2: home oxygen, help vs harm for lay users

**When it helps:** Documented low SpO2 (< 90-92%) from lung problems (COPD flare, pneumonia, heart failure with fluid in lungs) as a bridge while the ambulance comes. Oxygen treats low oxygen, nothing else: it does not relieve chest pain, treat a stroke, raise BP or sugar.

**Targets (British Thoracic Society, adopted by Indian bodies):**
- Most people: aim SpO2 **94-98%**.
- Known COPD or anyone told they "retain CO2": aim **88-92%**. Over-oxygenating can suppress breathing and cause dangerous CO2 build-up ([BTS Emergency Oxygen Guideline](https://www.brit-thoracic.org.uk/clinical-resources/guidelines/emergency-oxygen/)). Start COPD patients at 1-2 L/min by nasal cannula; others 2-4 L/min; adjust to hit the target, not higher.

**Pulse oximeter thresholds for the family plan** (MoHFW home-care rules and BTS):
- >= 94% at rest: normal, no oxygen.
- 90-93%: phone the doctor; repeat after 5 min; start low-flow oxygen if breathless.
- <= 90%, or falling steadily, or any reading with blue lips/confusion: **ambulance + oxygen** ([MoHFW guidelines](https://nhsrcindia.org/sites/default/files/2021-06/Revised%20Guidelines%20For%20Home%20Isolation%20of%20Mild_Asymptomatic%20COVID-19%20Cases.pdf)).
- Oximeters misread with cold fingers, nail polish, tremor, poor circulation, and darker skin tones (can read 1-3% high). Warm the hand, sit still 30 seconds, treat the patient, not the number.

**Cylinder / concentrator safety:**
- No flames, agarbatti, diyas, gas stoves, smoking or heaters within 3 metres of oxygen in use. Put up a "No smoking, oxygen" sign.
- Keep cylinders upright, chained or in a stand; never near a stove or in a hot vehicle; away from oil, grease and Vaseline.
- Use the correct regulator/flowmeter; open the valve slowly; never force or oil the valve. Replace when the gauge is in the red (< 500 psi).
- Concentrator: 90-95% oxygen, needs stable electricity. With power cuts, keep a filled backup cylinder or an inverter. Clean the filter weekly.
- Ask the supplier to demonstrate; write the flow-rate rules on a card taped to the machine.

## Part 3: emergency numbers in India and how they really work

- **112 (ERSS):** Single national number for police, fire, ambulance; free from any phone; also the "112 India" app with GPS, SMS, and panic button. Answered at a state call centre which dispatches or hands over to the state ambulance service ([112.gov.in](https://112.gov.in/about)).
- **108:** Free emergency ambulance (state-run PPP: GVK EMRI, Ziqitza etc.). Basic and advanced life-support vehicles with an EMT; takes patients to the nearest government (sometimes empanelled private) hospital. [REGION-DEPENDENT] In some states 108 is routed through 112. **Test-call both from the parents' landline and mobile.**
- **102:** Mainly for pregnant women and infants, not cardiac/stroke emergencies.
- **Private:** [RED.Health (formerly StanPlus)](https://www.stanplus.com/) claims ~15-minute response in covered cities; [Ziqitza](https://zhl.org.in/) subscription ambulances in ~60 cities; Medulance, VMEDO aggregators. Most large private hospitals run their own ambulance line. Rs 1,500-10,000+ per trip.
- **Realistic response times:** 108 averages 13-20 min in cities, 17-37+ min rural, beyond 45 min in remote areas. **Plan for a 30-60 minute wait in a secluded location** and identify a neighbour with a car as a parallel option for stroke/MI (not for suspected spine or hip injury, or cardiac arrest).
- **What to tell the dispatcher:** (1) "Ambulance needed"; (2) exact location: village, landmark, nearest main road, gate colour, and send someone to the road to flag the ambulance; (3) age, sex, main problem in one sentence ("70-year-old man, chest pain 20 minutes, diabetic, hypertensive"); (4) conscious and breathing?; (5) callback number; (6) don't hang up until told. Mention blood thinners or previous stroke/heart attack.

## Part 4: go-bag and emergency folder

A **red folder + zip bag by the front door**, with a photocopy set as photos/PDF on each child's phone:

1. **One-page medical summary** (laminated): name, DOB, blood group, diagnoses, allergies, pacemaker/stents, usual BP and sugar range, family doctor and preferred hospital.
2. **Medication list** with doses and timing, plus **the actual medicine strips**.
3. **Recent reports:** last ECG, echo, HbA1c, creatinine, discharge summaries (12 months).
4. **ID and insurance:** Aadhaar copies; **Ayushman Vay Vandana card** (all citizens 70+ eligible for Rs 5 lakh/year cashless treatment regardless of income; apply via the Ayushman app or PMJAY portal, [PIB](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2082288)); private policy card and TPA helpline.
5. **Cash Rs 5,000-10,000** and a UPI phone. Private hospitals often demand a deposit.
6. **Emergency contact card** in wallet and on the fridge.
7. **Advance directive (living will), optional:** Since the Supreme Court's January 2023 clarification in *Common Cause v Union of India*, a living will can be signed before two witnesses and attested by a notary or gazetted officer ([IAPC](https://www.palliativecare.in/the-supreme-court-of-india-eases-the-process-for-advance-medical-directives-living-will-and-withdrawal-of-life-support-treatment/)). DNR in India is a hospital physician-led decision under the [ICMR 2020 consensus guidelines](https://www.icmr.gov.in/icmrobject/uploads/Guidelines/1724842969_icmr_consensus_guidelines_on__do_not_attempt_ijmr.pdf); there is no at-home DNR that ambulance crews follow. [UNCERTAIN] Enforcement varies widely between hospitals.
8. **Spare glasses, hearing-aid batteries, dentures case, change of clothes, phone charger, water bottle.**
9. **Glucometer + strips, BP monitor, pulse oximeter, glucose tablets, aspirin 150 mg x 2** in the bag.

## Part 5: CPR and AED for lay rescuers

- **Indian Resuscitation Council "Compression-Only Life Support" (COLS):** check response, shout for help, call 108/112, heel of hand on centre of chest, push hard and fast (100-120/min, 5-6 cm deep, full recoil), swap rescuers every ~2 minutes, don't stop until help arrives or the person moves ([IRC COLS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5702999/)). Matches AHA 2025 hands-only CPR; gasping counts as not breathing; no pulse check for lay rescuers.
- **AED:** If available, turn it on and follow the voice prompts; it will not shock unless needed.
- **Is a home AED worth it?** The only large trial (HAT, 7,000 post-MI patients) found **no survival benefit** from a home AED over CPR training alone ([ACC](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/articles/2021/03/01/01/42/understanding-the-value-of-aeds-at-home)). **Verdict:** an AED (~Rs 1-1.5 lakh) is reasonable **only if** both parents, a neighbour, and any helper are trained and physically able to do CPR; otherwise spend on a wearable SOS device, an oximeter, and CPR training for whoever is nearest. Get everyone in the household, including domestic help and a neighbour, through an IRC/AHA-affiliated 2-hour CPR class.

## Part 6: medication-specific pitfalls

| Situation | Do | Don't |
|---|---|---|
| Suspected heart attack | Chew ~300 mg plain aspirin if not allergic and no active bleeding/ulcer; call first | Give aspirin if it may be a stroke; give to someone unable to swallow |
| Nitroglycerin/sorbitrate | Only the patient's own prescribed tablet under tongue, sitting; max 3 doses 5 min apart | Give if faint/low BP, or sildenafil/tadalafil in last 24-48 h; give someone else's tablet |
| Low sugar, conscious | Glucose tablets/sugar water, recheck 15 min | Chocolate/biscuits first (too slow); insulin |
| Low sugar, unconscious | Recovery position, glucagon if available, ambulance | Anything in the mouth |
| Glucagon | [REGION-DEPENDENT] 1 mg injectable kits (GlucaGen HypoKit) are sold in India in larger cities/online (~Rs 2,500-4,000), need refrigeration. Ask the endocrinologist for a prescription and train family/helper to inject into the thigh | Assume a chemist stocks it on the day |
| BP >= 180/120 | Rest, recheck, call doctor; ambulance if symptoms | Extra/double BP tablets, sublingual nifedipine; stopping tablets abruptly |
| Sick-day rules (fever, vomiting, diarrhoea) | Keep drinking; check sugar 4-hourly; call doctor about metformin/SGLT2/diuretic (often paused during dehydration) | Continue metformin or SGLT2 drugs through vomiting without advice |
| Blood thinners (clopidogrel, warfarin, apixaban) | Tell ambulance/ER immediately after any fall or head bump | Extra aspirin on top |
| Painkillers | Paracetamol is the safe default | NSAIDs (diclofenac, ibuprofen, nimesulide) with BP/kidney issues without advice |
| Sedatives/sleeping pills | | Give to a confused or breathless person |

## Priority actions for the family

1. Print the **BE FAST, chest pain, low sugar, and oximeter threshold** cards near the phone; buy a pulse oximeter, BP cuff, glucometer, glucose tablets, plain aspirin.
2. **Pre-identify** (and drive to, once) the nearest 24x7 CT-scan hospital and the nearest cath-lab hospital; save their ambulance numbers in the hub config.
3. Test-call 112 and 108 from the house; record the actual response time and which hospital they would go to.
4. Arrange a **daily check-in call** and a **wearable SOS/fall button**; brief one neighbour with the folder and CPR basics.
5. Enrol for the **Ayushman Vay Vandana** card if either parent is 70+; assemble the red folder.
6. Get everyone nearby trained in **hands-only CPR**, then revisit the AED question.
7. Ask the parents' doctors for written **sick-day rules**, a glucagon prescription, and whether either has any CO2-retention risk that changes the oxygen target.
